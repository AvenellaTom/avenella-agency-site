Where AI can make your working day easier

Skip the grand transformation plan. Start with the boring bits.

By Tom Paisley, Founder & Strategic Director

← All insights

Most of what is written about AI at work is either a prophecy or a panic. Neither is much use on a Tuesday morning when you have forty emails and a proposal due.

So here is a more modest view. AI is very good at a specific kind of work: the repetitive, wordy, slightly tedious kind that takes up more of the week than anyone admits. Start there.

Four places it earns its keep

First drafts

A blank page is slow. A rough draft you can argue with is fast. Proposals, follow-up emails, job adverts, the outline of a presentation. You will rewrite most of it, and you will still finish sooner.

Finding things

Every business has knowledge trapped in old documents, shared drives and one long-serving colleague's head. A simple knowledge base that staff can ask questions of saves a surprising number of "does anyone know where..." messages.

Summarising

Meeting recordings, long email threads, the five-minute voice note from a client who prefers talking to typing. Turning those into a short summary and a list of actions is exactly the kind of job to hand over.

Repetitive admin

Sorting enquiries, tagging records, reformatting the same report every week. If you can describe the steps, there is a good chance it can be automated.

The best first AI project is the task everyone on the team quietly hates.

Where it does not help

It is worth being as clear about the limits.

  • Judgement. It can lay out options. Choosing between them is still yours.
  • Taste. It produces the average of what already exists. Distinctive work needs a person with an opinion.
  • Relationships. Clients can tell when they are talking to a template.
  • Anything you cannot check. If you would not spot a mistake, do not delegate it.

How to start without a strategy document

Pick one task. Something small, frequent and annoying. Try doing it with AI for a week and keep a rough note of the time it took before and after.

If it helped, write down how you did it so a colleague can do the same. If it did not, you have lost very little. Then pick the next one.

That is the whole method. A string of small, proven wins will take a team further than an ambitious roadmap nobody finishes.

The habits that make it work

The tools are the easy part. The difference between a team that gets real value and one that tries it twice and gives up is mostly habit.

  • Give it context. The more it knows about your business, your customer and the result you want, the better the output. A one-line request gets a one-size-fits-all answer.
  • Show it an example. Paste in something you were happy with last time. "Like this, for this client" is the most useful instruction there is.
  • Always edit. Treat what comes back as a junior colleague's first attempt. Useful, quick, and not going to a client without your eyes on it.
  • Save what works. When an instruction produces a good result, keep it somewhere the team can reuse it. That shared library is worth more than any individual tool.

Mind what you put in

One caution. Be deliberate about what goes into these tools. Client details, contracts, anything confidential or personal should only go into systems your business has checked and agreed to use.

Write a short, plain policy so nobody has to guess. Half a page covering which tools are approved, what must never be pasted in, and who to ask when unsure. It takes an hour and removes most of the risk.

When to build something proper

Off-the-shelf tools will carry you a long way. The moment to consider something built for you is when the same task comes up daily, involves your own documents or data, and more than one person needs to do it the same way.

That is where a knowledge base or a small automation pays for itself, because the saving repeats every day without anyone having to remember the trick.

A word on the team

People worry, reasonably, about what this means for their jobs. Be straight with them about why you are doing it. The aim is to get the dull work off their desks so the interesting work gets more of their attention.
